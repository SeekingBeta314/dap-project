## Surveys: phases/taxonomy, metrics, and recommended evaluation practice (Ortenzi et al. 2021)

### Takeaway
Ortenzi et al. split a handover into a **pre-handover** phase (communication, grasp planning, motion planning/control, agreement on *where/when*) and a **physical handover** phase (from first receiver contact to full giver release), and recommend a **minimal metric set**: success rate, total handover time, receiver's task-completion time (objective) plus fluency, trust, working-alliance Likert items (subjective). Strabala et al. 2013 give the classic "what / when / where" coordination structure.

### Cited Findings
- Ortenzi et al. organise the review by phase: "pre-handover, and physical handover"; Section III covers reasoning/actions *before* the physical exchange (communication, grasping, motion planning and control), Section IV the physical exchange (grip modulation), Section V safety, Section VI metrics — [Ortenzi et al. 2021, arXiv:2007.12952 PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)
- The physical exchange starts when the receiver first contacts the object held by the giver and ends when the giver fully releases it; the review covers both robot-giver (R2H) and robot-receiver (H2R) roles, at cognitive (prediction, perception, planning, learning) and physical (motion, grasping, release) levels — [arXiv:2007.12952](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)
- Handovers are framed as **joint action**, needing shared representations, *prediction* of the partner's actions, and integration of predicted effects; a minimal joint-action architecture includes representations, monitoring (feedback), prediction (feedforward), and coordination — [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)
- Metric taxonomy: (1) task-performance metrics, (2) psycho-physiological metrics, (3) subjective questionnaires. "Success rate is the most popular task performance metric," but it "does not explain why and how the errors have occurred," and differing protocols make success rates hard to compare across papers — [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)
- Time metrics seen in surveyed papers: robot and human waiting time, total handover time, timing of the different phases, human reaction time, task-completion time. Fluency should be measured with objective metrics: **percentage of concurrent activity, human idle time, robot idle time, robot functional delay** (citing Hoffman & Breazeal 2007; Hoffman 2019) — [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)
- **Proposed minimal metric set**: "1) Task performance (objective): success rate, total handover time, receiver's task completion time. 2) Experience of the human (subjective): fluency, trust in robot, working alliance." Psycho-physiological measures are left out because they are hard to standardise. The authors give Likert items, e.g. "The human-robot team worked fluently together", "I trusted the robot to do the right thing at the right time" (a subset of Hoffman 2019's items) — [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)
- The authors also argue for a *post-handover* task for the receiver as part of evaluation, and note most H2R papers use a single object class — [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)
- Handover location: human-human handovers happen "roughly midway between giver and receiver" (citing Hansen et al.); interpersonal distance affects location and the height of the exchange; object mass affects duration, not location (citing Hansen et al. and Nemlekar et al. 2019) — [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)
- Strabala et al. propose a structure for human-robot handovers that separates the physical process from the social-cognitive one, coordinating "the what, when, and where": agreeing that a handover will happen, establishing its timing, and deciding the configuration where it happens — [Strabala et al. 2013, JHRI (S2 record)](https://doi.org/10.5898/JHRI.2.1.Strabala)
- In a learned intent-to-handover predictor trained on an instrumented-kitchen study, "mutual gaze and inter-personal distance ... were not key discriminative features" — [Strabala et al. 2012, RO-MAN](https://doi.org/10.1109/ROMAN.2012.6343875)
- Hoffman 2019 codifies subjective and objective fluency metrics, gives an analytical model for four objective metrics, and reports a user study linking objective and subjective fluency — [Hoffman 2019, IEEE THMS](https://doi.org/10.1109/THMS.2019.2904558)

### Inferences
- The dap maps onto the **pre-handover phase** only: no object, so no grip-release phase. The physical-handover phase becomes the palm-to-palm "touch" event. Ortenzi's split justifies scoring the dap on the approach and meeting moment.
- For a purely kinematic sim with a scripted partner, the Ortenzi subjective items cannot be collected. The objective part of the minimal set maps to: success rate; total time (start of partner motion to touch); and an analogue of "robot functional delay / robot idle time" (e.g., time between the partner reaching the meeting point and the robot palm arriving).
- Ortenzi's critique of bare success rate supports reporting a **failure-mode breakdown**, as HandoverSim does (below).

### Gaps
- Ortenzi's full table of per-paper metrics (Table in Sec. VI) was not transcribed. I did not read Strabala 2013's empirical timing results (the abstract has no numbers).
- **Micelli et al. "Perception and control challenges for effective human-robot handoffs" could not be verified** in Crossref; treat as UNVERIFIED.

## Reactive vision-based H2R handover systems and their controllers

### Takeaway
Classical reactive H2R systems are modular: perception (hand/body segmentation and object point cloud) → grasp generator (GG-CNN, 6-DOF GraspNet, learned grasp planners) → a reactive controller (PBVS in Rosenberger; Riemannian Motion Policies plus a symbolic state machine in Yang 2021; sampling-based MPC in Yang 2022; local tracking/global replanning switching in Marturi). Several of them fix the target once chosen or wait for the hand to stop. Few handle a target that keeps moving.

### Cited Findings
- **Rosenberger et al. (RA-L 2021):** single gripper-mounted RealSense D435 (640x480 @ 30 fps); YOLOv3 object detection; human body-part and hand/finger segmentation; GG-CNN grasp selection. Grasp candidates within ~5 px of human pixels are removed by erosion — [arXiv:2006.01797](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01797)
- Rosenberger control: "We use Position-Based Visual Servoing ... to drive the robot to the grasp pose" with velocity commands to the Panda controller. The handover point is the mean over a **5-frame window** (frames >7 cm from the mean discarded), and "Once chosen, the grasp pose stays the same until the handover is completed". The pipeline takes 0.0625 s per image, giving a "minimal handover initiation time of 0,19563 seconds". The arm "slows down significantly during the last 7cm" for safety; it aborts if the distance deviates or a body part comes near — [arXiv:2006.01797](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01797)
- Rosenberger results: 4 users (the developers), 13 YCB food objects, 520 handovers, **81.9% success**. Outcomes were labelled Success / Safety Stop (5.8%) / Grasping Fail (10.8%) / Detection Fail (1.5%) — [arXiv:2006.01797](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01797)
- **Yang et al. (ICRA 2021):** external Azure Kinect plus body tracking; hand/object segmentation; a **temporally consistent 6-DOF GraspNet**. The task model is a symbolic planner (Robust Logical Dynamical Systems) with 4 states: wait, approach a stand-off pose, grasp, drop. "Motions are implemented using Riemannian Motion Policies (RMPs)" with fast collision checks, and RRT-Connect when a straight line fails — [arXiv:2011.08961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.08961)
- Yang 2021 grasp selection: a cost trades grasp score against distance to the previous best approach pose and to home (w_s=1, w_q=0.1, w_home=5, w_prev=5), using Trac-IK. The stand-off pose is 10 cm back along the grasp axis and is updated at "roughly 10Hz". The final grasp is open-loop. In the user study (N=6, 10 objects), average approach time was **10.7 ± 3.6 s** and average success **81.8%** — [arXiv:2011.08961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.08961)
- **Yang et al. (ICRA 2022) MPC:** replaces the RMP motion layer with **sampling-based stochastic MPC** in joint space (joint-acceleration decision variables, 2 s horizon, motions at 50–100 Hz). Costs include goal, a "straight line" cost on gripper velocity direction, collision, and joint limits. It adds a learned grasp-reachability (manipulability) model and a neural F/T contact classifier. Versus Yang 2021, MPC cut approach time from 11.5 s to 7.1 s and total time from 13.3 s to 7.9 s, and raised success from 85.7% to 88.3%; 3 of 4 users preferred it — [arXiv:2204.00134](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.00134)
- Perception rates in Yang 2022: body tracking at 15 Hz, hand segmentation at 9 Hz, grasp generation at 5 Hz — [arXiv:2204.00134](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.00134)
- **Marturi et al. (Autonomous Robots):** the arm follows and grasps objects moved arbitrarily by a human. Learned-planner grasps are evaluated online for feasibility and collision-free reachability. The method switches dynamically between "a local planner, where the hand smoothly tracks the object, maintaining a steady relative pre-grasp pose" and "a global planner" that jumps to a new grasp when the current one becomes unreachable — [Marturi et al., S2 abstract of DOI 10.1007/s10514-018-9799-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10514-018-9799-1)
- Timing/legibility studies (robot as giver): Huber et al. 2008 compared a trapezoidal joint-space velocity profile with a minimum-jerk end-effector profile. Human reaction time was significantly shorter with minimum jerk (secondary summaries; full text not read) — [Huber et al. 2008 (TUM portal)](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/human-robot-interaction-in-handing-over-tasks/); the same claim is in [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952) ("Minimum-jerk motions by a robotic giver also result in shorter reaction time and faster adaptation for human receivers")
- Ortenzi's survey says most real H2R approaches are "over-conservative" in avoiding contact with the human to compensate for perception noise — [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)

### Inferences
- The project's **reactive differential-IK servo baseline** is closest to Rosenberger's PBVS, but should keep re-targeting (Rosenberger freezes the target pose once chosen). Marturi's "local tracking while keeping a steady relative pre-grasp pose" is the closest classical analogue to servoing a palm toward a moving, re-targeting partner palm. It is also a natural baseline variant: track a stand-off point, then commit.
- Yang 2022 suggests a cheap, strong non-learned baseline: sampling-based MPC over a short horizon with a straight-line/smoothness cost. It is a fair "privileged-state planner" comparison for the RL policy.
- None of these classical systems was designed for a partner moving at 0.15–0.9 m/s toward an unknown meeting point with mid-course switches. Most wait at a stand-off pose or assume the hand slows down.

### Gaps
- I did not read Cakmak et al. 2011 (spatial/temporal contrast) or Strabala/Micelli timing numbers in full text, so no quantitative timing results are reported from them.
- Marturi's quantitative results and control rates were not read (abstract only).

## Learned handovers and benchmarks (HandoverSim, Christen 2023, GenH2R, SynH2R, MobileH2R)

### Takeaway
The learned-H2R line (NVIDIA/ETH/Tsinghua) uses **egocentric wrist-camera segmented point clouds** and is evaluated on **HandoverSim**: success = gripper holds the object and brings it into a goal region; failures = contact / drop / timeout (13 s); plus execution and planning time. Training is either (a) **RL with planner demonstrations and a two-stage teacher-student scheme** (Christen 2023), or (b) **large-scale imitation/distillation from a privileged-state planner** into a point-cloud policy with a future-forecasting auxiliary loss (GenH2R, MobileH2R). The key evaluation split is **sequential** (robot waits until the hand stops) vs **simultaneous** (both move from t=0).

### Cited Findings
**HandoverSim (Chao et al., ICRA 2022)**
- Giver motion is replayed from **DexYCB** motion capture: 20 YCB objects and MANO hand models in PyBullet, giving 1,000 scenes. Each pre-handover sequence is "slightly less than 3 seconds" (pickup to offering), and the hand then holds still. Actions are target joint positions for a PD controller (7-DoF arm + 2-DoF gripper) — [arXiv:2205.09747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09747)
- **Success** = gripper fingers in contact with the object, gripper link inside a spherical goal region in front of the robot, both "continuously for 0.1 seconds". **Failure** = robot touches the human hand ("contact"), object dropped ("drop"), or the 13 s limit is reached ("timeout"). Episodes end at the first success or failure — [arXiv:2205.09747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09747)
- **Metrics**: efficacy (success rate and the failure rate for each cause), efficiency (mean completion time over *successful* episodes only, split into execution time and planning wall-time), and safety (the "contact" failure rate) — [arXiv:2205.09747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09747)
- Evaluation splits: S0 default, S1 unseen subjects, S2 unseen handedness, S3 unseen grasping — [arXiv:2205.09747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09747)
- **Baselines on S0**: OMG Planner (open-loop, waits for the hand to stop, ground-truth pose) 62.50% success and 9.722 s total; Yang et al. 2021 64.58%, 4.900 s; GA-DDPG "hold" 50.00%; GA-DDPG "w/o hold" 36.81%. The authors report a positive correlation with a real 6-participant user study — [arXiv:2205.09747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09747); numbers also tabulated in [Christen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17592)
- GA-DDPG w/o hold failures "are often caused by the gripper contacting the human hand or object when the hand and object are still actively moving, since the policy was never trained to adapt to moving objects" — [arXiv:2205.09747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09747)

**Christen et al., CVPR 2023 ("HandoverSim2real")**
- Observations: wrist-mounted RGB-D → hand and object point clouds using **ground-truth segmentation in simulation**, resampled to a fixed size, one-hot hand/object flags, encoded by **PointNet++**, then a 2-layer MLP that outputs a **6-DoF end-effector pose change**. The last visible point clouds are kept when the target is occluded. A separate learned **grasp predictor** decides when to switch to an open-loop grasp — [arXiv:2303.17592](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17592)
- Training: **TD3**, sparse reward (1 on success). **Two-stage teacher-student**: (1) pretraining in the *sequential* setting, alternating OMG-planner expert demos and RL exploration; (2) finetuning in the *simultaneous* setting, where the planner is replaced by a **frozen copy of the pretrained policy as "expert"**. Loss = λ·L_BC + (1−λ)·L_DDPG + L_AUX, where the auxiliary loss predicts the grasp goal pose. Naive RL finetuning without the expert "leads to an immediate drop in performance". Training took about 72–96 h — [arXiv:2303.17592](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17592)
- Results (HandoverSim s0): sequential **75.23%** (vs GA-DDPG 50.00%, finetuned 57.18%); simultaneous **68.75%** (vs 36.81% / 54.86%). Sim-to-real pilot (2 subjects × 10 objects × 6 poses): 41/60 vs 21/60 and 41/60 vs 33/60 against GA-DDPG. A 6-user study used Likert ratings and found the timing "more appropriate" — [arXiv:2303.17592](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17592)

**GenH2R (CVPR 2024)**
- GenH2R-Sim: procedurally generated scenes (1,000,000 training scenes, 3,266 objects) with hand-object trajectories built from chained **Bézier curves** with randomised control points and hand speed, 8 s motions. A "t1" test set comes from HOI4D mocap — [arXiv:2401.00929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)
- **Distillation-friendly demonstrations from a privileged planner**: the OMG planner is given privileged object 6D pose, candidate grasps, and hand pose. A "foresighted" planner (plans to the privileged final state) yields demos that "requires accurately forecasting the end state", which hurts distillation. A "shortsighted" planner that replans every step gives zig-zag, multi-modal actions. Their **landmark planning** replans periodically toward future landmarks that an object-pose forecaster *can* predict ("only considers visually foreseeable futures") — [arXiv:2401.00929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)
- Student: **forecast-aided 4D imitation learning** on egocentric point clouds (PointNet++ with ICP-based motion/flow features), plus an auxiliary objective forecasting future object poses — [arXiv:2401.00929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)
- New metric **AS (Average Success)** = ∫₀¹ Success(t) dt, where Success(t) is the success rate counting only successes within t·T_max. It combines success and speed, like AP — [arXiv:2401.00929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)
- Results: trained on t0 with landmark planning, **86.57%** (s0 sequential) and **85.65%** (s0 simultaneous). Sim-to-real (6 users, 5 objects): 90.0% vs 56.7% (simple) and 70.0% vs 33.3% (complex) against HandoverSim2real — [arXiv:2401.00929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)
- Protocol caveat raised by GenH2R: "HandoverSim2real manually makes their policy hold still in the first 1.5 seconds in the code implementation, deviating from the simultaneous setting definition". Re-run in the true simultaneous setting, it falls to 25.69% (s0-trained) — [arXiv:2401.00929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)

**SynH2R (ICRA 2024)** — synthesises handover-friendly hand-object grasping motions, giving "100x more objects than previous work". Trained purely on synthetic data, it is competitive with methods that use real mocap — [arXiv:2311.05599](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.05599)

**MobileH2R (CVPR 2025)** — extends to a mobile base: synthetic **full-body** human motion; automatically generated "safe, imitation-friendly" demonstrations whose planner avoids collisions with the human body; and 4D imitation learning that also takes the **human body point cloud** as input. It reports at least +15% success over baselines, and says scaled demonstrations reduce collisions "by about 1/3" and raise success by 11.6% — [arXiv:2501.04595](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04595)

### Inferences
- **Closest template for the project's privileged teacher → depth student plan is GenH2R/MobileH2R**: a privileged-state expert distilled into a point-cloud (depth-derived) policy, plus an auxiliary forecasting loss. GenH2R's warning applies directly: a teacher that uses privileged *future* information (e.g., the partner's true meeting point or switch time) creates demos the student cannot imitate from observations. The teacher should act only on information that is "visually foreseeable" from the history. For the dap this means the teacher should *not* see the post-switch target before the switch is observable.
- Christen's recipe (RL with BC regularisation toward a frozen expert, sequential→simultaneous curriculum) is a ready-made fallback if pure RL from depth is unstable. "Partner holds still at meeting point" → "partner moving" is the dap's analogue of sequential → simultaneous.
- Adopt HandoverSim-style reporting: a success rate; failure rates by cause (here likely: wrong-orientation / not antiparallel, miss / timeout, "collision" or overshoot past the partner palm); time over successes only; and GenH2R's AS curve as a single success-and-speed number. Also report a **sequential (partner static) vs simultaneous (partner moving)** split, and state precisely whether any initial hold or delay is in the code.
- These benchmarks use **wrist cameras plus ground-truth segmentation in sim**. A head-mounted depth camera with no segmentation is a different (harder or easier depending on occlusion) observation model, and this should be stated when comparing.

### Gaps
- HandoverSim's simulation control rate and GA-DDPG's 0.15 s step were seen only in passing. I did not check HandoverSim's goal-region radius.
- MobileH2R's exact metrics and baselines table were not transcribed.
- I found no handover benchmark that uses a **head-/third-person depth camera** with RL from raw depth pixels. All of the learned H2R works above use segmented point clouds.

## Human reach / motion prediction for HRI: models, and how early the endpoint can be predicted

### Takeaway
Minimum-jerk (Flash & Hogan 1985) is the standard generative model of point-to-point reaching (straight path, bell-shaped speed). For **unconstrained handover reaches**, however, it fits the *path shape* poorly (Chan et al. 2020). Endpoint/target prediction methods (time-series classification, GMM/GP, ProMP, DMP, min-jerk fitting) typically reach usable accuracy after **~1/3 to 2/3 of the motion**: ≥70% target classification in the first third (<500 ms) for discrete targets (Pérez-D'Arpino & Shah); classification of handover configuration at ~43% of the trajectory (Lang et al.); improved OTP accuracy in the first ~45% of the motion (Nemlekar et al.); min-jerk end-position error under 10% only after ~2/3 of the motion (Li & Hauser).

### Cited Findings
- Flash & Hogan: the objective is the integrated squared magnitude of hand jerk. Predictions confirmed experimentally for planar movements: "unconstrained point-to-point motions are approximately straight with bell-shaped tangential velocity profiles". The model works only "when formulated in terms of the motion of the hand in extracorporal space" — [Flash & Hogan 1985 (S2 abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.05-07-01688.1985)
- Chan et al. (Humanoids 2020) fit 1,200 unconstrained human-human handover reaches (20 participants, Vicon) to Minimum Jerk (straight-line path), Decoupled Minimum Jerk, and Conic models. Best fit: Conic 62.9%, Decoupled MJ 37.0%, Minimum Jerk **0.08%** (1 sample). Conic fits were 34.1% elliptical, 59.0% hyperbolic, 6.9% parabolic. Note the fit is to **path geometry** (closest-point error), not the speed profile — [arXiv:2108.12780](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.12780)
- Chan et al. also found 26.03% of givers first bring the object toward themselves before reaching out (a speed trough), versus 73.97% who reach directly — [arXiv:2108.12780](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.12780)
- Pérez-D'Arpino & Shah 2015: library of demonstrations with "each time step ... encoded as a multivariate Gaussian distribution" over arm DOFs (time-series classification). They report "70% or higher correct classification on average for the first third of the trajectory (< 500msec)", plus a PR2 proof-of-concept and an analysis of how task-level priors improve early prediction — [Pérez-D'Arpino & Shah 2015 (S2 abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2015.7140066); [MIT DSpace record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/106656)
- Mainprice & Berenson 2013: a GMM over a human-motion library predicts workspace occupancy as the **swept volume** of regressed motions. The planner minimises penetration cost while interleaving planning and execution, and stays safe "even in cases where the initial prediction of the human's motion is incorrect" (abstract via search snippets) — [search result summary; Crossref DOI 10.1109/IROS.2013.6696368](https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.2013.6696368)
- Follow-ups: inverse optimal control for reaching prediction (Mainprice, Hayne, Berenson, ICRA 2015 and T-RO 2016), and unsupervised early prediction (Luo, Hayne, Berenson, Auton. Robots 2017) — [Crossref DOI 10.1109/TRO.2016.2581216](https://doi.org/10.1109/TRO.2016.2581216); [DOI 10.1007/s10514-017-9655-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10514-017-9655-8)
- Maeda et al. ProMPs: an imitation-learned mixture of human-robot interaction primitives lets the robot's assistive trajectory be inferred from human observations (action recognition plus coordination). It was evaluated with a lightweight arm, including a coordinated bottle handover — [Maeda et al. 2016, Auton. Robots (S2 abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10514-016-9556-2). Phase estimation for fast recognition: [Maeda et al. 2017 IJRR](https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364917693927) (content not read)
- Nemlekar et al. 2019: Object Transfer Point predictor fusing (1) an **offline OTP from human preferences** and (2) a **dynamic OTP from observed motion**. It is more accurate "at the earlier phase of handover (up to 45% of the handover motion)" than either alone, gives a reach-to-grasp response time of about **3.1 s**, "close to natural human receiver's response", and uses a user-adaptive reference frame — [Nemlekar et al. 2019 (S2 abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2019.8794008)
- Li & Hauser 2015: 10 subjects × 280 3D reaches. With a **minimum-jerk** fit, "the prediction error ratio of end position is reduced to less than 10% after two thirds of the motion has been observed". A history-based linear regression predicts end *position* earlier but end *time* much worse. They note Glasauer et al. combined a min-jerk estimate with "a naïve estimate approximately centered between the giver and receiver" — [Li & Hauser 2015 PDF](https://users.wpi.edu/~zli11/papers/W2015_RSS_Hauser_Handover.pdf) (venue UNVERIFIED)
- Lang et al. 2017: GPs with stochastic trajectory classification on dual-quaternion 6D hand motion classify the handover configuration "at an average of 43.4% of the trajectory" — [arXiv:1707.02745](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.02745)
- Other predictors surveyed by Ortenzi: DMPs + EKF for handover location and time (Widmann & Karayiannidis 2018); min-jerk with a semi-adaptable neural network (Landi et al. 2019); GMM/GMR two-layer framework (Luo et al.) — [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952); DOIs in table above

### Inferences
- The partner model (min-jerk point-to-point to a near-midpoint target) is well-grounded in Flash & Hogan for the *speed profile* and in Hansen / Glasauer / Nemlekar for the *midpoint location prior*. Chan et al. 2020 is a **citable limitation**: real unconstrained handover reaches are curved (conic or decoupled-MJ), so a straight-line min-jerk partner is a simplification. A decoupled-MJ variant (z finishing earlier than xy) would be a cheap robustness test. This is a suggestion only; no experiments were run.
- The literature figures (~30–45% of the motion for usable target or OTP prediction; ~2/3 for <10% min-jerk endpoint error) imply that a reactive servo which waits for the endpoint leaves most of the reach unused. A learned policy that implicitly predicts the endpoint (or a min-jerk-fit predictor baseline) should gain mainly in the *first half* of the reach. Mid-course target switches reset that clock.
- Nemlekar's fusion of a static prior with a dynamic estimate maps directly to a dap baseline: a midpoint prior blended with an online min-jerk endpoint fit.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain Pérez-D'Arpino & Shah's full per-time-step accuracy curve or the PR2 task-time results (abstract only); the full-text PDF link returned 404.
- No source found that measures endpoint-prediction latency for **mid-course target switches** (double-step reaching) in an HRI handover setting. The motor-control double-step literature was outside scope and was not searched.
- Mainprice & Berenson 2013 quantitative results were not read.

## Timing and fluency metrics in handovers and joint action

### Takeaway
The standard objective fluency metrics (Hoffman) are **human idle time, robot idle time, concurrent activity (%), and robot functional delay**, with standard Likert fluency, trust, and working-alliance items. Handover-specific timing metrics are total handover time, approach time, number of attempts, and waiting times. Benchmarks report execution time over successes and, lately, a success-vs-time integral (AS).

### Cited Findings
- Objective fluency metrics: "percentage of concurrent activity, human idle time, robot idle time and robot functional delay" — [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952), citing [Hoffman & Breazeal 2007](https://doi.org/10.1145/1228716.1228718) and [Hoffman 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/THMS.2019.2904558)
- Hoffman 2019 gives an analytical model for four objective fluency metrics and links them to subjective fluency in a user study — [Hoffman 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/THMS.2019.2904558)
- Handover time metrics in practice: approach time, number of grasp attempts, success rate (Yang 2021, user study mean approach 10.7 s); approach and total time (Yang 2022: 7.1 s / 7.9 s with MPC); execution and planning time over successes (HandoverSim); "minimal handover initiation time" (Rosenberger, about 0.196 s) — [arXiv:2011.08961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.08961); [arXiv:2204.00134](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.00134); [arXiv:2205.09747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09747); [arXiv:2006.01797](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01797)
- AS (Average Success) metric integrates success over normalised time budgets — [GenH2R, arXiv:2401.00929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)
- Human-receiver benchmark: Nemlekar's robot gets a reach-to-grasp response time of about 3.1 s, "close to natural human receiver's response" — [Nemlekar et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2019.8794008)
- A robot giver moving with minimum jerk shortens human reaction time (Huber et al. 2008, via secondary summaries and Ortenzi) — [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952); [TUM portal record](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/human-robot-interaction-in-handing-over-tasks/)
- Pan et al. evaluated social perception of H2R handovers with RoSAS (warmth, competence, discomfort); Ortenzi notes Pan et al. found real-time motions with deliberate delays affect perceived quality (detail not verified in primary) — [Pan et al. HRI 2018, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3171221.3171257); [Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)

### Inferences
- For a scripted partner, the computable fluency analogues are: (i) **robot functional delay**, the time from the partner reaching the meeting point to contact (zero or negative is ideal); (ii) **partner idle time**, the time the partner palm waits at the endpoint; (iii) **concurrent motion %**, the share of the partner's reach during which the robot also moves toward it. Together with success rate, time-to-touch over successes, and an AS-style curve, these match the Ortenzi minimal objective set and HandoverSim reporting.
- Contact-quality metrics specific to the dap (palm normal antiparallelism error, relative approach speed at contact) have no direct handover analogue. The nearest are HandoverSim's "contact" safety failure and the interaction-force metrics in Ortenzi. They should be defined explicitly as task-specific.

### Gaps
- Exact operational definitions and formulas of Hoffman's four metrics were not read from the primary (only Ortenzi's listing).
- No quantitative human-human handover duration statistics were extracted from a primary source. HandoverSim's DexYCB pre-handover sequences are "slightly less than 3 seconds" but those are pickup-to-offer motions, not reach durations alone.
