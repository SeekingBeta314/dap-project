# Six research lines converge on the robot dap

No published work does what the dap does, but six literatures each solve one piece of it, and together they give the project a clear methods lineage and a sharp claim to novelty. Classical catching (Bäuml 2010, Kim 2014, Salehian 2016) supplies prediction and **explicit intercept point-and-time selection**. Visual servoing and damped-least-squares IK (Whitney 1969, Nakamura & Hanafusa 1986, Chaumette & Hutchinson 2007) supply the low-level controller. Handover research (Rosenberger 2021, Yang 2021/2022, HandoverSim, GenH2R) supplies standoff-then-close controllers, benchmark protocols and the closest teacher-student precedents. Reach-prediction work (Flash & Hogan 1985, Pérez-D'Arpino & Shah 2015) grounds the min-jerk partner. Social-touch work (Fitter & Kuchenbecker, Christen et al. 2019) gives the task framing, and legged/drone teacher-student work (Agarwal 2022, Robot Parkour 2023) gives the training recipe. The reactive servo baseline is a **textbook, defensible moving-target position-based visual servo (PBVS)**. It skips the one step every landmark catcher includes, choosing *when* to meet, and the early runs show exactly that signature: contacts landed **1.6 s early to 0.6 s late**, a third of them after the partner had stopped. The planned depth-pixel teacher-student policy appears to be the first arm controller to map raw head-camera depth to joint commands for meeting a moving target with an orientation constraint. I found no verified precedent, but that is absence of evidence from non-exhaustive searches. Its success will hinge on two things. First, giving the teacher only information the student can infer. Second, evaluating on timing and contact quality and on out-of-distribution splits, because the servo already saturates binary success at 12 seeds.

## Each literature solves one slice of the dap problem

The dap decomposes into perception of a moving partner palm, prediction of where and when it will be, choice of a meeting pose and time, and control of a 7-DoF arm to get there with the right orientation. No single body of work covers all four. The table maps each body to its landmark papers, its core method, and what the dap should borrow. Bibliographic details flagged in the appendix should be checked before they go into the write-up.

### Catching established the four-stage interception pipeline

Every classical catcher runs the same pipeline. It predicts the object's trajectory, selects an intercept point *and time* under a reachability model, generates a trajectory to that point re-planned at sensor rate, and executes through joint-level control. Bäuml et al. merge "where, when and how to catch" into one nonlinear program over catch configuration and catch time. They re-plan every **20 ms** from an EKF and explicitly compensate a measured **90 ms camera-to-motion delay**, which gives **>80% catch rates** on balls thrown at about 7 m/s with about 1 s of flight ([Bäuml et al. 2010](https://elib.dlr.de/67146/1/B%C3%A4uml2010.pdf)). Kim et al. learn object dynamics with SVR and model the reachable and graspable spaces with GMMs. They drive the hand with a learned dynamical system whose output is "converted into the 7-DOF joint state using the damped least squares IK" at 500 Hz, and catch **52 of 71 valid throws (73.2%)** on a KUKA LWR, against 38% for untrained humans ([Kim et al. 2014](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/198748/files/TRO2316022.pdf?version=1)). Kim's failure analysis matters for the dap. **12 of 19 failures** came from a Cartesian target the arm could not reach in time, because the task-space generator ignored joint velocity limits ([Kim et al. 2014](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/198748/files/TRO2316022.pdf?version=1)). Salehian et al.'s "soft catching" is the closest classical analogue to an antiparallel palm touch. On a KUKA iiwa with an Allegro hand, the palm is constrained to "face opposite the trajectory of the object" and matches its velocity at contact, which raised success to **70–75%** where hard catching managed 0–5% ([Salehian et al. 2016](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/217007/files/TRO.pdf)). Earlier systems used parabola fits with recursive least squares and chose the intercept closest to the base or the initial end-effector position ([Kim et al. 2014](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/198748/files/TRO2316022.pdf?version=1)). Croft, Fenton & Benhabib formalized optimal rendezvous-point selection ([Croft et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1109/3477.662759)); only the bibliographic record was checked, not the content. These systems face 5–7 m/s objects with under a second of flight. The dap's 0.15–0.9 m/s partner is an order of magnitude slower, which is why a purely reactive servo can work at all.

### Visual servoing supplies the textbook moving-target control law

The canonical tutorials define PBVS and IBVS as resolved-rate laws. Chaumette & Hutchinson's Part I explicitly assumes "a motionless target" ([Chaumette & Hutchinson 2006, HAL](https://inria.hal.science/inria-00350283/document)). Part II adds the moving-target case: a feedforward term −L̂⁺∂ê/∂t "must be introduced in the control law to compensate for the target motion". Without an accurate estimate of that term "tracking errors will be observed". The listed remedies are integral action, filters that "smooth or predict the motion", and predictive control ([Chaumette & Hutchinson 2007, HAL](https://inria.hal.science/inria-00350638/document)). The underlying inverse is classical. Resolved-rate control dates to Whitney ([Whitney 1969](https://doi.org/10.1109/TMMS.1969.299896)). The singularity-robust or damped-least-squares inverse comes from Nakamura & Hanafusa ([1986](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.3143764)) and Wampler ([1986](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSMC.1986.289285)), and Chiaverini, Siciliano & Egeland review it ([1994](https://doi.org/10.1109/87.294335)). Drake's `DoDifferentialInverseKinematics` instead poses diff-IK as a QP with a direction-preserving scale α∈[0,1], subject to J·v = α·V and joint position, velocity and acceleration limits ([Drake docs](https://drake.mit.edu/doxygen_cxx/group__planning__kinematics.html); [Tedrake, *Robotic Manipulation*](https://manipulation.csail.mit.edu/pick.html)). The closest historical precedent in speed regime is Allen et al.'s tracking and grasping of a moving model train at "approximately human arm movement rates" with predictive filtering ([Allen et al. 1993](https://doi.org/10.1109/70.238279)). Its content is UNVERIFIED: only abstract-level summaries were seen.

### Handover research contributes controllers, benchmarks and the closest learned precedents

Reactive human-to-robot handover systems have a structure close to the dap servo. Rosenberger et al. "use Position-Based Visual Servoing … to drive the robot to the grasp pose" from a gripper-mounted D435. They freeze the grasp pose once chosen, slow down in the last 7 cm, and achieve **81.9% success over 520 handovers** ([Rosenberger et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01797)). Yang et al. drive the gripper to a **10 cm standoff pose updated at roughly 10 Hz** with Riemannian Motion Policies, then execute an open-loop grasp, reaching **81.8%** success ([Yang et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.08961)). Their follow-up replaces RMPs with **sampling-based MPC over a 2 s horizon at 50–100 Hz**, which cuts approach time from 11.5 s to 7.1 s and raises success from 85.7% to 88.3% ([Yang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.00134)). HandoverSim sets the benchmark protocol. Success means continuous grasp contact and goal-region presence for 0.1 s, and failures are categorized as contact, drop or 13 s timeout. Baselines include the OMG planner (**62.50%**), Yang 2021 (**64.58%**) and GA-DDPG without hold (**36.81%**), which failed because it "was never trained to adapt to moving objects" ([Chao et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09747)). On the learned side, Christen et al. train a PointNet++ policy on wrist-camera point clouds (with ground-truth segmentation in simulation) using TD3. Their two-stage teacher-student scheme fine-tunes against a frozen pretrained expert with a behavior-cloning regularizer, reaching **75.23% sequential and 68.75% simultaneous** success ([Christen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17592)). GenH2R distills a privileged planner into a point-cloud policy with a future-forecasting auxiliary loss. It warns that demonstrations planned toward the privileged final state "require accurately forecasting the end state" and hurt distillation, so its planner only "considers visually foreseeable futures" ([Wang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)).

### Reach prediction grounds the partner model and its limits

Minimum jerk is the standard model of point-to-point reaching: "approximately straight with bell-shaped tangential velocity profiles" ([Flash & Hogan 1985](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.05-07-01688.1985)). It is a weaker model of handover paths. Fitting 1,200 unconstrained handover reaches, Chan et al. found the straight-line minimum-jerk path best for only **0.08%** of samples, against 62.9% for conic paths ([Chan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.12780)). Endpoint prediction becomes usable partway through a reach. Pérez-D'Arpino & Shah report "70% or higher correct classification on average for the first third of the trajectory (< 500msec)" ([2015](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2015.7140066)). Lang et al. classify the handover configuration at **43.4%** of the trajectory ([2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.02745)). Nemlekar et al. fuse a static preference prior with a dynamic estimate and gain accuracy "up to 45% of the handover motion" ([2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2019.8794008)). Li & Hauser get minimum-jerk endpoint error below 10% only "after two thirds of the motion has been observed" ([Li & Hauser 2015](https://users.wpi.edu/~zli11/papers/W2015_RSS_Hauser_Handover.pdf); venue UNVERIFIED).

### Social touch work frames the task but never solved its perception problem

Fitter & Kuchenbecker's Baxter hand-clapping line is the landmark for robot clapping. It used kinesthetically taught keyframes played back on a beat, wrist-worn IMUs at 200 Hz to recognize human motions (**85.9% live accuracy**), and no vision at all ([Fitter & Kuchenbecker 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frobt.2018.00085); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/how-high-fives-help-us-get-in-touch-with-robots)). Users found tempo highly salient ([Fitter & Kuchenbecker 2020, IJSR](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/ijsr/FitterK20.html)). Fitter herself described the interaction as "somewhere between a high five and a hand-clapping game" ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/how-high-fives-help-us-get-in-touch-with-robots)). The learned branch uses interaction primitives: a joint distribution over human and robot trajectories, conditioned on the observed partial human motion ([Ben Amor et al. 2014](https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/interaction-primitives-for-human-robot-cooperation-tasks)). This line extends to Bayesian IPs, which estimate phase and trajectory jointly ([Campbell & Ben Amor 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v78/campbell17a.html)), and to MILD, which benchmarks high fives and fist bumps captured with a D435 but reports only trajectory MSE ([Prasad et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.12418)). Jindai et al.'s handshake robot shapes its approach as "second-order lag and dead time elements" driven by the human hand trajectory, which makes it a reactive tracker in effect ([Jindai et al. 2008](https://www.fujipress.jp/jrm/rb/robot002000040650/)). The single closest precedent for an RL "dap arm" is Christen, Stevšić & Hilliges. They trained DDPG for a Shadow hand on a 4-DoF arm to clap, handshake and fingertip-touch, using rewards weighted from human mocap. The policy observed **target-hand link positions, not pixels**, the target was mostly static, and removing the contact reward hurt success ([Christen et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1906.11695)).

### Learned interception still routes perception through a state estimator

Learned table tennis and catching systems consistently feed a small policy a low-dimensional estimated target state, never raw pixels. Google DeepMind's table tennis controllers see an 8-step history ("0.14 seconds") of ball state from a 125 Hz stereo tracker. They were trained with evolution strategies because PPO and SAC gave "noticeably jerkier" actions, and the system won **45% of matches** against humans ([D'Ambrosio et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.03906); venue UNVERIFIED). Agile Catching gives the cleanest learned-versus-model-based comparison. A blackbox policy and a whole-body SQP/MPC both catch **80–85%** of mechanical throws, but on human throws the MPC holds **68.9%** while the policy collapses to **2.0%** ([Abeyruwan et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.08205v2)). Catch It! trains PPO in two stages. Its first stage counts success when "the palm touches the object in flight", a criterion close to the dap's, and real-robot palm touch reached **65–75%** with a low-pass filter on actions but only 5–15% without one ([Zhang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.10319); venue UNVERIFIED). The badminton legged manipulator uses an asymmetric actor-critic: the actor sees the shuttle position plus a detection flag under a noise model fitted to real camera data, and only the critic gets privileged state. This handles a **60–160 ms** perception delay ([Ma et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22974)).

### Privileged teacher-student learning is the established route to depth policies

The recipe has three steps. Train a teacher with RL on compact privileged state. Distill it into a sensor-based student with on-policy DAgger ([Ross et al. 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)). Give the student memory. Learning by Cheating showed the payoff: one-stage imitation reached **20%** success, while the two-stage privileged scheme reached **100%** ([Chen et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12294)). Agarwal et al. train on scandots for **15 billion samples (13 h)**, then distill into a depth ConvNet-GRU for **6 million samples (6 h)** at 58×87 resolution, accounting for the depth camera's latency in phase 2. They state that direct RL on depth "would require billions of samples" ([Agarwal et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07638)). Robot Parkour's ablation is the starkest. Direct PPO from depth scored **0/0/73/0/100%** across five skills, a memoryless MLP student 0/1/63/43/100%, and the distilled GRU student **86/80/100/73/100%**, against privileged oracles at 95/82/100/100/100% ([Zhuang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05665)). The one dynamic-environment teacher-student precedent is perception-aware drone flight, which distills a full-state RL teacher into a depth-image student ([Song et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.01841)). Its content was seen only through search summaries and its venue is UNVERIFIED.

## The servo is textbook moving-target PBVS, minus intercept-time selection

### It is a standard, defensible baseline when described as PBVS

Every component of the baseline has a landmark citation, and the assembled architecture appears in published handover systems. The 10 ms loop with a constant-acceleration Kalman filter and partner-velocity feedforward is exactly Chaumette & Hutchinson's moving-target PBVS: the feedforward term plus a filter that "smooth[s] or predict[s] the motion" ([Chaumette & Hutchinson 2007](https://inria.hal.science/inria-00350638/document)). Damped-least-squares resolved rate is the standard low-level layer ([Whitney 1969](https://doi.org/10.1109/TMMS.1969.299896); [Nakamura & Hanafusa 1986](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.3143764); [Wampler 1986](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSMC.1986.289285); [Chiaverini et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1109/87.294335)), and Kim et al. use DLS IK inside a landmark catcher ([Kim et al. 2014](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/198748/files/TRO2316022.pdf?version=1)). The 5 cm standoff that closes along the palm normal mirrors Yang et al.'s 10 cm standoff and approach ([Yang et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.08961)) and Rosenberger's PBVS with a slowed final 7 cm ([Rosenberger et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01797)). The antiparallel target mirrors Salehian's palm "opposite the trajectory of the object" ([Salehian et al. 2016](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/217007/files/TRO.pdf)). One framing caution: learned-policy papers rarely compare against "pure diff-IK". They use motion planners (OMG), reactive RMP systems, or MPC ([Chao et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09747); [Yang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.00134)). Calling the baseline a "diff-IK controller" invites a straw-man critique, while calling it a predictive PBVS places it correctly. A description the team can adapt:

> Our classical baseline is a position-based visual servo on a moving target (Chaumette & Hutchinson 2007). Every 10 ms, a constant-acceleration Kalman filter predicts the partner palm 0.1 s ahead to compensate sensing and actuation latency (cf. Bäuml et al. 2010). The commanded pose is antiparallel to the predicted palm, held at a 5 cm standoff and then closed along the palm normal (cf. Yang et al. 2021). Joint velocities come from damped-least-squares resolved-rate control (Whitney 1969; Nakamura & Hanafusa 1986; Wampler 1986) with velocity scaling and partner-velocity feedforward.

If the implementation calls Drake's `DifferentialInverseKinematics` rather than an explicit DLS pseudo-inverse, cite the Drake QP formulation and Tedrake's notes instead. Those notes do not use the term "damped least squares" ([Tedrake](https://manipulation.csail.mit.edu/pick.html)). The 0.1 s look-ahead should be justified as **latency compensation**, as in Bäuml's 90 ms ([Bäuml et al. 2010](https://elib.dlr.de/67146/1/B%C3%A4uml2010.pdf)), not as intercept planning.

### The early timing spread is the signature of a missing rendezvous choice

Every landmark catcher, from Hove & Slotine through Bäuml, Kim and Salehian, explicitly picks a reachable intercept point and time. The servo instead chases a fixed 0.1 s-ahead prediction and touches whenever the arm catches up. The early runs show the predicted consequence. Contacts ranged from **1.6 s before to 0.6 s after** the partner's meeting time, and **about a third came after the partner had stopped**. In Hoffman's fluency vocabulary, that third is positive robot functional delay plus partner idle time ([Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952); [Hoffman 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/THMS.2019.2904558)). The early contacts mean the robot met a palm still moving mid-reach rather than at its endpoint. Two further mechanisms deepen the lag. First, a constant-acceleration model is biased on a min-jerk profile, whose acceleration changes continuously, and Chaumette & Hutchinson state that feedforward error produces tracking error ([2007](https://inria.hal.science/inria-00350638/document)). Second, Drake-style direction-preserving scaling silently slows the arm when the commanded twist is infeasible; this is Kim's dominant failure mode in another guise ([Kim et al. 2014](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/198748/files/TRO2316022.pdf?version=1)). The perception split is also telling. On wide layouts the servo scored **11/12 with the stand-in pose sensor but 12/12 with ground truth**, which echoes Bäuml's finding that vision, not the arm, was the main failure source ([Bäuml et al. 2010](https://elib.dlr.de/67146/1/B%C3%A4uml2010.pdf)). At 12 seeds, though, one failure cannot establish this.

### Four upgrades would make the baseline harder to dismiss

The literature points to a small set of stronger classical comparators. Each adds a piece the RL policy must otherwise learn implicitly. None has been tested here.

Two diagnostics cost almost nothing and preempt reviewer questions. Log Drake's α (or the DLS residual) per step, and plot palm tracking error against time, which Chaumette & Hutchinson's analysis predicts will be non-zero during the jerk phases. Because the partner follows a min-jerk reach, its future path is highly predictable once the endpoint is estimated. That makes a rendezvous planner relatively more powerful here than in ballistic catching. This is an inference; no paper tests it in this setting.

## Pixel-to-joint interception with a distilled depth student has no verified precedent

### The novelty is the combination, not any single component

PPO teachers, DAgger students, frame stacks and depth CNNs are all standard. What no source in the notes does is combine them for an arm meeting a moving target. Every learned catching and table tennis system routes perception through a separate tracker that outputs a low-dimensional state ([D'Ambrosio et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.03906); [Abeyruwan et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.08205v2); [Zhang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.10319)). The learned handover line uses wrist-camera point clouds with ground-truth segmentation in simulation, not head-camera depth without segmentation ([Christen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17592); [Wang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)). The closest social-touch RL work observed privileged target-hand positions and a mostly static target ([Christen et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1906.11695)). Teacher-student depth distillation is proven for locomotion over static terrain ([Agarwal et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07638); [Zhuang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05665)) and for drones ([Song et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.01841), content via summaries only), not for arm contact with a moving partner. The research notes also found no paper that compares learned interception directly against a reactive visual-servo or diff-IK baseline on an arm. They found no verified end-to-end pixel RL for social touch gestures (from roughly 30 searches) and no standard simulation benchmark for high-five or dap meetings. The defensible novelty claim has three parts. First, raw head-camera depth to joint velocities for meeting a moving partner palm. Second, an orientation constraint (antiparallel within 15°) rather than position-only contact; Catch It!'s palm-touch criterion ignores orientation. Third, a paired, ground-truth comparison against classical servos. Phrase it as "to our knowledge" because the searches were not exhaustive.

### A skeptic would ask why RL is needed at all

The steelman objection is strong. The partner moves at 0.15–0.9 m/s. One frame of latency is about 33 ms if the camera runs at 30 fps, gentler than badminton's 60–160 ms ([Ma et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22974)). And the servo already scores 12/12 on nominal layouts. The best counter-argument comes from drone racing. RL "optimizes a better objective", while optimal control splits planning from control through an intermediate trajectory that "limits the range of behaviors" ([Song et al. 2023, Science Robotics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10943)). For the dap, the objective (antiparallel contact at the partner's meeting time, at low relative speed) is awkward to express as a servo error but direct to express as a reward. The project's hypothesis should therefore be that RL improves **timing and contact quality**, not that it raises binary success. Agile Catching gives the matching warning. Learned policies win in-distribution and are "less robust to distribution shifts", in their case collapsing from about 80% to 2.0% on human throws ([Abeyruwan et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.08205v2)). In a purely kinematic simulation, the usual model-based advantages (no real data needed, robustness to dynamics mismatch) shrink. Robustness to distribution shift remains, and mid-course target switches and speed extremes are where to test it.

## The privileged-learning literature dictates what the teacher may see

### Give the teacher only the delayed, current palm state

The most consequential design choice is the teacher's observation. When a teacher acts on information the student cannot infer, DAgger converges to the teacher's policy averaged over that hidden information. Weihs et al. call this the "imitation gap" and show its size "directly impacts agent performance" ([Weihs et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12173)). Warrington et al. put it this way: "the expert does not know what the trainee cannot see" ([2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15566)). Walsman et al. call such teachers "impossibly good" ([ICLR 2023](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2023/poster/11786)). GenH2R found the same thing in handovers: demonstrations aimed at the privileged final state hurt distillation ([Wang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)). For the dap, the teacher should see the partner palm pose and velocity **with the same one-frame delay** the student has. This is our design choice; the nearest precedent is Agarwal et al.'s "Noisy" elevation-map baseline, a teacher-student pair for which they "simulate a latency of 40ms in both the phases of training" (Sec. 3, Baselines; their main scandot teacher states no latency) ([Agarwal et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07638)). It should not see the sampled speed profile, the reach endpoint, or the switch time before the switch is visible. Agarwal chose privileged inputs "such that phase 2 loss is low" and bounds student suboptimality by the teacher-student action gap ([2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07638)). If richer teacher inputs prove necessary, the literature's fixes are adaptive IL/RL weighting ([TGRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03186); [ADVISOR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12173)). Alternatively, follow DAgger with PPO fine-tuning that uses an asymmetric critic ([Pinto et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06542)) and keeps a behavior-cloning term toward the frozen teacher. Christen et al. report that naive fine-tuning without the expert "leads to an immediate drop in performance" ([Christen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17592)). The same logic applies to the oracle baseline. If it uses future partner information, report it as an upper bound, not as a fair comparator.

### Memory, small images and depth noise are the student defaults

Every successful student in the reviewed set has memory. Lee et al.'s TCN-100 student had **35.5% lower deviation** than TCN-1 ([Lee et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11251)). Robot Parkour's memoryless MLP student fell well below the GRU student ([Zhuang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05665)). A 3-frame stack follows DrQ-v2's convention for inferring "velocity and acceleration" ([Yarats et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.09645)), but a GRU on top is cheap insurance against mid-course switches. Image sizes in the depth-locomotion literature are small: 58×87 (Agarwal, Extreme Parkour) and 48×64 (Robot Parkour, with a CNN of 16/32/32 channels feeding a 256-unit GRU) ([Agarwal et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07638); [Cheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14341); [Zhuang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05665)). The dap's 212×120 frames have about five times the pixels of 58×87, so downsampling is consistent with practice; this is arithmetic, not a cited result. Robot Parkour simulates "depth clipping, pixel-level Gaussian noise, and random artifacts" and randomizes latency, camera pose and field of view ([Zhuang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05665)). DrQ/RAD-style random shifts regularize pixel learners ([Kostrikov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13649); [Laskin et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14990)). Applying the same shift across all stacked frames preserves motion cues; that is common practice but not separately cited. Extreme Parkour initializes the student actor from the phase-1 network "to minimize the drift" ([Cheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14341)). LbC warm-starts with offline behavior cloning before on-policy DAgger because DAgger is slower ([Chen et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12294)). Near contact, the robot's own arm can occlude the partner palm from the head camera. Christen et al. keep the last visible point cloud, and the badminton work feeds a detection flag ([Christen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17592); [Ma et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22974)). Finally, watch action smoothness. Google chose ES over PPO for smoother actions, and Catch It!'s low-pass filter was decisive on hardware ([D'Ambrosio et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.03906); [Zhang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.10319)). An action-rate penalty in the teacher will also make it easier to distill.

### Distillation, not pixel RL from scratch, fits a laptop budget

Direct PPO from depth failed in Robot Parkour ([Zhuang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05665)), and Agarwal estimates billions of samples ([2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07638)). If the team wants a pixel-RL-from-scratch comparison arm, off-policy augmented learners such as DrQ-v2 are better supported than PPO. DrQ-v2 solves most DeepMind Control tasks in about 1–3M frames ([Yarats et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.09645)). The research notes' throughput arithmetic, an inference rather than a source, puts things in scale. At about 95 depth steps/s/core across about 10 cores, an Agarwal-sized 6M-step DAgger phase takes about 1.75 h of simulation. At about 620 privileged steps/s/core, 50M teacher steps take about 2.2 h. Teacher budgets of 10⁷ rather than locomotion's 10⁹ are plausible for a kinematic arm with 20–100-step episodes, but this is unverified.

## Ground-truth timing metrics and harder splits would fill the field's evaluation gap

### Success rate is already saturated and underpowered

With **12/12** successes, an exact binomial (Clopper-Pearson) 95% interval still admits a true success rate as low as about 74%; this is my arithmetic, not a cited figure. **11/12 versus 12/12** cannot separate any two methods. Ortenzi et al. warn that success rate "does not explain why and how the errors have occurred" ([Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)). The evaluation should therefore lead with the continuous, paired measures the project already computes: contact-time error relative to the partner's meeting time, antiparallel rotation error, position offset, and relative contact speed. Contact speed is the natural safety proxy, since a kinematic simulation models no force. It should also add more seeds and paired tests on those measures. The social-touch literature measures timing mostly through user ratings and trajectory MSE, and the notes found no standardized contact-timing-error metric for high fives or claps ([Prasad et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.12418); [Fitter & Kuchenbecker 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frobt.2018.00085)). Ground-truth timing and orientation metrics are therefore a contribution in themselves.

### Borrow failure taxonomies, time-integrated success and fluency analogues

HandoverSim reports success, failure rates by cause, and completion time over successful episodes only ([Chao et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09747)). Rosenberger labels outcomes as success, safety stop, grasp failure or detection failure ([Rosenberger et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01797)). The dap analogues are wrong orientation, position miss, timeout, and overshoot or excessive contact speed. GenH2R's **Average Success**, ∫₀¹ Success(t)dt over normalized time budgets, folds success and speed into one number ([Wang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)). Hoffman's objective fluency metrics translate directly to a scripted partner ([Ortenzi et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12952)). Robot functional delay becomes the time from the partner reaching its meeting point to contact. Partner idle time and concurrent motion are the other two analogues. These directly quantify the servo's "third of contacts after the partner stopped".

### Split the evaluation to separate perception, control and generalization

The baseline ladder already supports a clean decomposition, and the literature suggests making it explicit. Servo-with-ground-truth against the privileged teacher compares control strategies with perception removed. Servo-with-stand-in-sensor against the depth student compares whole systems, but the two see different inputs. State the stand-in sensor's noise model, or add a servo variant driven by the same depth stream, so the comparison is not confounded. Teacher against student prices the perception and imitation gap, as Robot Parkour's oracle-versus-distilled columns do (for example, tilt **100% → 73%**) ([Zhuang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05665)). For generalization, follow Agile Catching and report out-of-distribution splits separately: speed extremes near 0.15 and 0.9 m/s, wide layouts, and mid-course target switches ([Abeyruwan et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.08205v2)). Also adopt HandoverSim's sequential (partner static) versus simultaneous (partner moving) split, and heed GenH2R's protocol warning. A prior method secretly held still for 1.5 s, and its simultaneous score fell to **25.69%** when re-run faithfully, so any initial hold or delay in the code must be documented ([Wang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00929)). Finally, name the partner model's limits. Real handover reaches are mostly curved, with straight-line minimum jerk fitting **0.08%** ([Chan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.12780)), so a decoupled-minimum-jerk partner is a cheap robustness check. And user studies found arm stiffness changes perceived safety ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/how-high-fives-help-us-get-in-touch-with-robots)), which a kinematic simulation cannot capture.

## Conclusion

The literature recasts the project's central question. RL does not need to prove it can touch the partner palm, because a 1990s-style predictive PBVS already does that nearly every time. The open question is whether a learned policy can choose *when* to meet, the step every landmark catcher solves explicitly with a rendezvous planner and the step the servo omits. That makes an intercept-aware or MPC servo the comparator that decides whether the RL result means anything, and it makes timing error, contact speed and robot functional delay the headline metrics rather than success rate.

The teacher design is where the project is most likely to succeed or fail quietly. The handover, locomotion and imitation-gap literatures converge on one rule: a teacher is only as useful as the student's ability to infer what it saw. A dap teacher that knows the partner's endpoint or switch time will produce an "impossibly good" policy whose distilled student averages over futures it cannot see. That failure would look like exactly the timing scatter the servo already shows. Keeping the teacher information-myopic, giving the student memory, and testing on switches and speed extremes are what turn a plausible novelty claim, pixels to joint velocities for oriented contact with a moving partner, into a defensible one.
